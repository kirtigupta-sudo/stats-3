Start with the business question

How sure are we about the average?

A business rarely measures every customer or every order. A sample gives us a useful estimate—but a different sample would give a different answer. This lesson starts with that practical problem and builds toward confidence intervals, one visible step at a time.

One sample gives one estimate. Repeated samples reveal its uncertainty.

01
The decision before the formula

Why do we need a confidence interval?

Imagine a retailer deciding whether to promise faster delivery. The operations team wants the true average order preparation time, but a full audit of every order is too expensive. They take a sample.

A sample is one possible snapshot

Suppose one random sample of 25 orders has an average preparation time of 42 minutes. If we sampled 25 different orders, we probably would not get exactly 42 again. Sampling variability means estimates naturally change from sample to sample—even when the population has not changed.

The number 42 is not necessarily the exact population average. It is our point estimate from this sample. We need to express how much sampling uncertainty surrounds it.

Why not report 42 ± one standard deviation?

Standard deviation describes spread among individual order times, not uncertainty in the estimated average. If orders vary a lot, the average can still be estimated quite precisely when the sample is large.

Individual-order spread: s = 10 minutes
Uncertainty in sample mean: SE = s / √n
For n = 25: SE = 10 / √25 = 2 minutes

Reporting 42 ± 10 would address individual variability, not form a confidence interval for the population mean.

Point estimate

One number from the sample used to estimate a population parameter. Here, x̄ = 42 minutes estimates the unknown mean μ.

Uncertainty

The estimate would vary if we collected a different sample. Standard error measures typical sample-to-sample variation of the estimator.

Interval estimate

A range around the point estimate, built with a method designed to capture the true parameter at a chosen long-run rate.

Intuition: We do not know the exact population mean. We know our sample mean, how noisy that estimate is likely to be, and how cautious we want to be. A confidence interval combines those ingredients into a defensible range.
02
How the evidence is selected

Sampling techniques

A population is the complete group we want to understand. A sample is the subset we observe. A good calculation cannot rescue a sample that systematically leaves out important parts of the population.

Delivery-time example

Population: all 80,000 orders shipped last quarter
Sample: 600 observed orders
Parameter: μ = true mean delivery time of all orders
Statistic: x̄ = mean delivery time of sampled orders

A parameter describes a population and is generally unknown. A statistic is calculated from sample data. The sample statistic estimates the population parameter. n denotes sample size; N often denotes population size.

Sampling-frame warning

The sampling frame is the actual list or system from which we select observations. If remote-region deliveries are missing from the database, random sampling from that database will still miss them. Random selection cannot fix an incomplete frame.

Probability sampling

Simple random

Select units randomly from a complete list, giving each a known selection chance. Useful when a full frame exists.

Probability sampling

Stratified

Split into important groups—such as region—and sample within each. Helps guarantee that small but important groups are represented.

Probability sampling

Cluster

Randomly select natural groups, such as stores, then measure units inside them. Can reduce collection costs; similarity within a cluster can reduce precision.

Probability sampling

Systematic

Choose a random start, then select every k-th record. Watch for periodic patterns that align with k.

Non-probability sampling

Convenience

Take whoever is easiest to reach. Fast, but the people easiest to reach may differ from the population.

Non-probability sampling

Voluntary response

People opt in themselves. Those with especially strong experiences may respond more often, distorting the result.

Random sampling ≠ random assignment. Random sampling selects units from a population and supports generalization. Random assignment places study participants into groups and supports causal comparisons.
03
From noisy individual values to stable averages

CLT: why sample means become approximately normal

The Central Limit Theorem concerns the distribution of repeated sample means—not the shape of the original data. This distinction is essential.

Three different distributions

1

Population distribution: every individual order time. It might be right-skewed because a few orders take unusually long.

2

One sample's data: the observations in the 25 orders we measured. They may still look skewed.

3

Sampling distribution of x̄: means from many hypothetical samples of 25 orders. This is the distribution described by the CLT.

Why does averaging help?

A mean combines multiple observations. An unusually slow order may be balanced by more ordinary or faster orders. Across repeated samples, averages tend to cluster around the population mean. Under suitable conditions, that cluster becomes approximately bell-shaped as n grows.

“Large enough” depends on population skew and tails. Independent observations and finite variance matter. If the population itself is normal, the sample mean is normal for any sample size when observations are independent.

E(x̄) = μ    and    SD(x̄) = σ / √n
In practice, estimate SD(x̄) with SE = s / √n

Worked standard-error calculation

A sample contains n = 25 order times, with sample standard deviation s = 10 minutes.

SE = s / √n
SE = 10 / √25
SE = 10 / 5 = 2 minutes

The 10-minute SD describes individual order variability. The 2-minute SE describes estimated variation in the sample average across repeated samples.

Visual intuition: what does one standard error look like?

The curve below represents a hypothetical sampling distribution of sample means. The center is the true population mean. If this distribution is approximately normal, about 68% of sample means fall within ±1 SE, about 95% within ±1.96 SE, and about 99% within ±2.576 SE. These percentages describe sample means, not individual observations.

Teal = central 68% (about ±1 SE)  ·  Coral = additional area from ±1 to ±1.96 SE  ·  Black line = population mean

The 95% span is wider because it must cover more of the sampling distribution: it reaches nearly two standard errors in both directions. That same idea leads to the confidence-interval multiplier.

Interactive CLT simulation: increase n and watch means cluster

The simulated population is deliberately right-skewed. Each histogram shows averages from repeated samples. Increasing the number of observations per sample usually narrows the spread of those averages and makes their distribution more bell-shaped.

Sample size has diminishing returns: SE shrinks with the square root of n. To halve the SE, multiply sample size by four. Doubling n does not halve the SE.
04
How cautious should the interval be?

Critical values: reading z and t tables

A critical value tells us how many standard errors to extend on each side of the estimate. Higher confidence requires reaching farther into the tails, so the interval becomes wider.

Critical value as a multiplier

Margin of error = critical value × standard error
Interval = estimate ± (critical value × SE)

A critical value is a multiplier, not a distance measured in minutes. If SE is 2 minutes and the critical value is 2, the margin of error is 4 minutes.

For a two-sided 95% interval, the central area is 0.95. The remaining 0.05 is split equally between two tails: 0.025 in each. The z critical value has cumulative area 0.975 to its left, so z* ≈ 1.96.

Visual: larger critical value, wider interval

The standard error stays fixed. A 99% confidence target uses a larger multiplier than 90%, so its range extends farther from the estimate.

A larger critical value adds more standard errors to the margin of error.

How to use a t table when the exact df row is missing

  1. Calculate degrees of freedom: df = n − 1.
  2. Choose the column matching the confidence level and whether the question is one- or two-sided.
  3. If the exact df row is absent, a conservative classroom approach is to use the next lower df row; it gives a slightly larger critical value.
  4. As df grows, the t critical value approaches the matching z value.

Table-heading trap: tables may label columns by one-tail probability α/2, two-tail probability α, or confidence level. Check the heading before choosing a column.

05
Put the pieces together

Step-by-step: a t confidence interval

We have 25 randomly sampled orders. Their preparation times have a mean of 42 minutes and a sample standard deviation of 10 minutes. Estimate the population mean preparation time with a 95% interval.

1

Choose the method. The population SD σ is unknown, so use a one-sample t interval.

2

Calculate degrees of freedom. df = n − 1 = 25 − 1 = 24.

3

Calculate standard error. SE = s/√n = 10/√25 = 10/5 = 2 minutes.

4

Find the critical value. In the 95% two-sided t table, row df = 24, t* ≈ 2.064.

5

Calculate margin of error. ME = t* × SE = 2.064 × 2 = 4.128 minutes.

6

Calculate endpoints. Lower = 42 − 4.128 = 37.872. Upper = 42 + 4.128 = 46.128 minutes.

x̄ ± t* × s/√n
42 ± 2.064 × 10/√25
42 ± 4.128
95% CI = [37.872, 46.128] ≈ [37.9, 46.1] minutes

Under the sampling assumptions, the t-interval procedure captures the population mean in about 95% of repeated samples. This is an interval about the mean, not a range containing 95% of individual orders.

What if σ were genuinely known?

If the population SD were known to be 10 minutes, z* = 1.96 and SE = 10/√25 = 2:

ME = 1.96 × 2 = 3.92
z interval = 42 ± 3.92 = [38.08, 45.92]

It is narrower than the t interval because it does not account for estimating σ.

When is z appropriate?

For a mean, z is appropriate when σ is genuinely known and the sampling distribution is normal or well approximated by normal. If σ is unknown and estimated with s, the standard one-sample method is t.

The t method is straightforward and approaches z as degrees of freedom grow.

07
Interpret the result, not just calculate it

What does “95% confident” mean?

The confidence level describes the long-run performance of the procedure. It is not a statement about the proportion of individuals in an interval.

Imagine repeating the whole process

Draw a random sample, calculate an interval, and repeat many times. The true population mean stays fixed, but samples and intervals vary. A valid 95% procedure covers the true mean in approximately 95% of repetitions and misses in approximately 5%.

Not what it means

  • Not that 95% of individual orders fall inside the interval.
  • Not that the true parameter changes randomly after we calculate the interval.
  • Not a guarantee that this particular interval contains the truth.
  • Not protection from biased sampling, poor measurement, or missing groups.

Repeated-sample picture: some intervals miss

The black vertical line is the fixed population mean. Teal intervals contain it; coral intervals do not. These examples are illustrative: a small batch need not show exactly 95% coverage.

A 95% method is judged over many repetitions, not by demanding exactly 19 successful intervals in every set of 20.

Careful wording: “We are 95% confident that the population mean lies between these endpoints” is conventional shorthand. More precisely, the method used to create the interval captures the fixed population mean in 95% of repeated samples, assuming its conditions hold.
08
Keep the concepts separate

Beginner confusion traps

Understanding what the calculation does—and does not say—is as important as getting the arithmetic right.

“Sample mean ± standard deviation is a confidence interval.”

Usually not. SD describes individual-value spread. For a mean interval, use a standard error and a critical value.

“The CLT makes the original observations normal.”

No. It describes the sampling distribution of the mean under suitable conditions, not necessarily the raw data.

“Bigger n eliminates bias.”

No. Bigger samples reduce random error, but a biased or incomplete sampling process can remain biased at any size.

“A 95% interval contains 95% of the data.”

No. It is an interval estimate of a population parameter, not an interval for individual observations.

“Standard error is the typical distance of data points from the mean.”

That is closer to the role of SD. SE estimates sample-to-sample variation of a statistic.

“Higher confidence means greater precision.”

At the same sample size and variability, higher confidence means a larger critical value and a wider interval—more coverage, less precision.

“The t method is only for tiny samples.”

No. When σ is unknown, t is appropriate for means across sample sizes. Its critical value approaches z as df grows.

“A correct formula guarantees a valid conclusion.”

No. Sampling design, independence, measurement quality, and model assumptions matter too.

Checklist before reporting an interval

  1. What population and parameter are being estimated?
  2. How were observations selected? Who or what could be missing?
  3. Are observations independent, or are there store, customer, or time clusters?
  4. What are x̄, n, and s? Is σ genuinely known?
  5. Which critical value is appropriate, and how did the table lookup work?
  6. Can the result be interpreted as long-run coverage rather than a range containing most individual data?
