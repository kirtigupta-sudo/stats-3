STATISTICS · CLASS 3 Sampling → variation → uncertainty
A practical guide to inference

From a small sample to a careful estimate

You sampled 25 orders and got an average preparation time of 42 minutes. Is that the true average for every order? Probably not exactly. But how far might it be from the truth—and how should you communicate that uncertainty? This lesson builds the answer from the business question upward, then lets you explore how the calculations work.

The sample is evidence, not the whole population.

01
Start with the business decision

Why do we need a confidence interval?

A sample gives us an estimate, but it does not reveal the exact population value. The first question is not “Which formula?” It is “How much would this answer change if we had sampled different orders?”

Scenario: are orders taking too long?

A retailer has thousands of orders and wants to know the average time from order acceptance to ready-for-dispatch. Measuring every order is possible only with extra effort, so the analyst takes a random sample of 25 orders. Their average is 42 minutes.

The operations team needs to decide whether to add a shift. If the true average is about 42 minutes, one plan may make sense. If it is closer to 50 minutes, another might. Reporting only “42 minutes” hides an important fact: another random sample of 25 orders would probably produce a different average.

Why not just report the sample mean?

The sample mean is our best point estimate in this example, but it is only one number from one sample. We need an indication of its likely sampling variation. The interval adds a range around the estimate using the observed variability, the sample size, and a chosen confidence level.

Point estimate: x̄ = 42 minutes
Interval estimate: x̄ ± an uncertainty allowance

Why not use sample mean ± standard deviation?

The standard deviation describes the spread of individual orders, not how uncertain the sample mean is. If individual orders vary by 10 minutes, it does not follow that the estimated average is uncertain by only 10 minutes, nor does “42 ± 10” have a chosen confidence interpretation.

To describe uncertainty in the mean, first use the standard error s/√n, then multiply by a critical value for the desired confidence level. Each piece has a distinct job.

Sample mean: the center

Where this sample puts its best estimate. Here, x̄ = 42 minutes.

Standard error: sample-to-sample wobble

How much the sample mean would typically vary across repeated samples of the same size.

Critical value: coverage multiplier

How many standard errors to extend on each side to reach the selected long-run confidence level.

The key idea: an interval is not a claim that every value in the range is equally plausible. It is a range produced by a method designed to have a stated long-run capture rate for the population parameter, assuming the data and model satisfy the required conditions.

A preview: three samples, three answers

Imagine three teams independently sample 25 orders. Their sample averages might be 39, 42, and 46 minutes. None automatically equals the population mean. The interval method adds a principled uncertainty range around each average instead of pretending that a single sample value is exact.

02
How observations enter the analysis

Sampling techniques

A population is the full group we want to understand. A sample is the subset we actually observe. Sampling is not just a cost-saving step: the method used to select the sample affects whether its results can represent the population.

Business example: delivery performance

Suppose a retailer wants the average delivery time for all 80,000 orders shipped last quarter. Measuring every order may be inconvenient, so an analyst selects 600 orders and calculates the sample mean. The 80,000 delivery times are the population; the 600 observed times are the sample.

Population: N = 80,000 orders
Sample: n = 600 observed orders
Parameter: μ = true average delivery time for all 80,000 orders
Statistic: x̄ = average delivery time among the 600 sampled orders

A parameter describes a population and is usually unknown. A statistic is calculated from a sample and is used to estimate that parameter. The symbol n denotes the sample size.

The sampling frame matters

A sampling frame is the practical list or system from which the sample is drawn: for example, the order database. If canceled orders or remote-region shipments are missing from the frame, a perfectly random draw from that incomplete list still will not represent all orders.

A representative sample reflects the important features of the target population. Random selection can reduce selection bias, but it cannot repair a badly defined population or an incomplete frame.

Common ways to select a sample

Simple random sampling

Every population member has a known, equal chance of being selected. Use a random-number generator to choose order IDs.

Useful when: you have a complete list and no subgroup needs a guaranteed minimum.

Stratified sampling

Split the population into meaningful groups called strata—such as region—and randomly sample within every group.

Useful when: you need each region represented. A sample can be proportional to group size or deliberately oversample small groups.

Cluster sampling

Split the population into natural clusters—such as stores—randomly choose some clusters, then observe all or some members inside them.

Useful when: collecting data across a wide area is expensive. It is often cheaper, but similar people within a cluster can reduce precision.

Systematic sampling

Choose a random starting point, then select every kth entry from an ordered list. For 600 of 60,000 orders, k = 100.

Watch for: a repeating pattern in the list, such as entries sorted by a cycle that aligns with every 100th record.

Convenience sampling

Take observations that are easiest to reach, such as customers who answered an optional pop-up survey.

Risk: respondents may differ from nonrespondents. A large convenience sample can still be biased.

Voluntary-response sampling

People choose for themselves whether to participate, often after seeing an open invitation.

Risk: people with strong opinions may be much more likely to respond, so the sample can exaggerate extreme views.

Random sampling is not random assignment. Random sampling chooses people or units from a population and helps with generalization. Random assignment places participants into treatment groups and helps with causal comparisons. A study may use one, both, or neither.
03
Why sample averages behave predictably

The Central Limit Theorem and standard error

Individual observations can be skewed or irregular. The Central Limit Theorem (CLT) explains why averages from repeated random samples often form an approximately normal distribution, which makes interval estimation possible.

First separate three distributions

1

Population distribution: the values of every unit in the group, such as every customer's order value. It may be skewed.

2

Sample data distribution: the observed values in one sample. It may also be skewed, especially when the sample is small.

3

Sampling distribution of the mean: the distribution of sample means from many repeated samples of the same size. The CLT describes this distribution—not necessarily the raw data.

If samples of size n are repeatedly drawn:
E(x̄) = μ    and    SD(x̄) = σ / √n

Why does averaging help?

A sample mean combines many observations. Unusually high and low values partly balance one another, so means tend to vary less than individual observations. As n grows, the distribution of sample means becomes more concentrated around the true population mean μ.

Under the CLT, that sampling distribution becomes approximately bell-shaped when the sample is sufficiently large, provided observations are independent and the population has finite variance. How large is “sufficient” depends on how skewed or heavy-tailed the population is.

If the population itself is normal, the sample mean is normally distributed for any sample size (with independent observations). The CLT is most useful when the population is not normal.

Standard deviation (SD)

Describes the spread of individual values in the population or sample. It measures typical observation-to-observation variation.

Standard error (SE)

Describes the spread of a statistic across repeated samples. For a sample mean, it estimates how much x̄ typically changes from sample to sample.

Sample size effect

SE shrinks in proportion to 1/√n. To halve SE, you need roughly four times as many observations—not twice as many.

Known population SD:   SE(x̄) = σ / √n
Unknown population SD:   estimated SE(x̄) = s / √n

Here σ is the population standard deviation and s is the sample standard deviation. We usually do not know σ, so in real business analysis we estimate the standard error using s. This substitution adds uncertainty, which the t-distribution accounts for.

Worked SE example: average support response time

A random sample of n = 64 support tickets has a sample mean response time of 12.4 hours and sample SD s = 8 hours.

SE = s / √n
SE = 8 / √64
SE = 8 / 8 = 1 hour

The 8-hour SD describes variation among individual ticket times. The 1-hour SE describes the estimated variation of the sample average across repeated samples of 64 tickets. It does not mean every ticket is within one hour of the sample mean.

Visual intuition: what does one standard error look like?

The center line represents the true mean in a hypothetical world. If the sampling distribution is approximately normal, about 68% of sample means fall within ±1 SE, about 95% within ±1.96 SE, and about 99% within ±2.576 SE. These percentages concern sample means, not individual observations.

approximately 68% within ±1 SE additional area to approximately 95% within ±1.96 SE center at population mean
Important assumptions: the sample should be selected in a way that supports the intended inference; observations should be independent, or dependence should be handled; and the sample size should be adequate for the population's shape. Repeated customers, stores, households, or time-series records can create dependence.
04
Interpreting uncertainty correctly

What a 95% confidence interval actually means

A confidence interval is a range produced by a method that captures an unknown population parameter at a known long-run rate—if the method's assumptions hold.

The repeated-sampling interpretation

Imagine taking many new random samples of the same size, and building a 95% confidence interval from each one using the same procedure. In the long run, approximately 95% of those intervals would contain the fixed true population mean. About 5% would miss it.

The population mean is fixed; the sample and interval change from sample to sample. Before sampling, the method has a 95% coverage rate. After calculating one interval, we do not ordinarily say there is a 95% probability that the fixed mean is inside that specific interval.

What it does not mean

  • It does not mean 95% of individual customers or orders lie inside the interval.
  • It does not mean 95% of sample means lie inside this one interval.
  • It does not mean the parameter changes randomly after the interval is computed.
  • It does not guarantee the interval contains the truth in this particular study.
  • It does not correct bias from poor sampling, nonresponse, bad measurement, or missing groups.

Confidence level

The confidence level is the long-run success rate of the interval procedure. A higher level, such as 99% rather than 95%, requires a wider interval to capture the parameter more often.

Confidence interval

The particular lower-to-upper range computed from one sample. It depends on the sample estimate, observed variability, sample size, and chosen confidence level.

05
From a target confidence to a multiplier

Critical values: what they are and how tables give them

A critical value tells us how many standard errors to go from the estimate in each direction. Its size depends on the confidence level and on whether we use the standard normal z distribution or the t distribution.

Build the idea using a two-sided 95% interval

A standard normal curve has total area 1. For 95% confidence, we want the central 0.95 area. That leaves 0.05 outside the central region. Because the interval is two-sided, we split that tail area equally: 0.025 in each tail.

Confidence level = 0.95
Total tail area α = 1 − 0.95 = 0.05
Area in each tail = α/2 = 0.025
Cumulative area to upper cutoff = 1 − 0.025 = 0.975
z* with cumulative area 0.975 = 1.96

So the central 95% of a standard normal distribution runs from −1.96 to +1.96. The positive cutoff, 1.96, is the two-sided 95% z critical value.

What does “critical” mean?

It is the boundary between the central region we keep and the tail probability we leave outside. A larger critical value puts the boundary farther from the center, producing a wider interval.

A critical value is not the same thing as a data value. The z critical value 1.96 means “1.96 standard errors from the center” on the standard normal scale. To return to the original units, multiply it by the standard error.

How to read a t table

A t table is organized by degrees of freedom (df), usually in rows, and by a tail probability or confidence level in columns. For a one-sample mean, df = n − 1. Unlike the normal table, a t critical value depends on both confidence level and df.

For a two-sided 95% interval with n = 25, df = 24. In a table headed by two-tail probability α, use α = 0.05 and row df = 24. In a table headed by one-tail probability, use 0.025 instead.

Estimate ± uncertainty allowance

Computing a confidence interval

The basic structure is the same for both the z and t methods: sample estimate ± margin of error. The methods differ in which reference distribution supplies the critical value.

Confidence interval = estimate ± margin of error
Margin of error (ME) = critical value × standard error
For a mean: x̄ ± critical value × (standard error of x̄)

Method A: z interval

Use the z method for a population mean when the population SD σ is known, and the sampling distribution of the mean is normal or well-approximated by normality. The standard normal critical value for a two-sided 95% interval is approximately 1.96.

x̄ ± z* × σ / √n
95% two-sided interval: x̄ ± 1.96 × SE

The value 1.96 leaves 2.5% in each tail of the standard normal curve. The central 95% spans from z = −1.96 to z = +1.96.

Method B: t interval

Use the one-sample t method for a population mean when σ is unknown and estimated by the sample SD s. It uses the t-distribution with degrees of freedom df = n − 1.

x̄ ± t* × s / √n
df = n − 1

The t curve has heavier tails than the normal curve, especially for small samples. That produces a larger critical value and a wider interval, reflecting the extra uncertainty from estimating σ with s.

Worked example: average order preparation time

A sample of n = 25 orders has mean preparation time x̄ = 42 minutes and sample SD s = 10 minutes. We want a two-sided 95% interval for the population mean, and σ is unknown. Therefore use a t interval with df = 25 − 1 = 24.

1

Calculate the standard error: SE = s/√n = 10/√25 = 10/5 = 2 minutes.

2

Find the t critical value: for 95% confidence and df = 24, t* ≈ 2.064.

3

Calculate the margin of error: ME = t* × SE = 2.064 × 2 = 4.128 minutes.

4

Build the interval: 42 ± 4.128 = [42 − 4.128, 42 + 4.128] = [37.872, 46.128] minutes, or about [37.9, 46.1].

Interpretation: using this sampling procedure, the 95% t-interval method would capture the true mean preparation time in about 95% of repeated samples, under its assumptions. This interval is not a range containing 95% of individual order times.

Why is t wider than z?

In the worked example, a z approximation would use 1.96 instead of 2.064: ME = 1.96 × 2 = 3.92, giving [38.08, 45.92]. The t interval is wider because the sample SD is only an estimate of population variability. As degrees of freedom grow, the t critical value approaches 1.96.

Choosing z or t in practice

For a mean, use t when σ is unknown—which is the ordinary real-world case. Use z when σ is genuinely known from reliable external information. Large-sample z approximations are sometimes used, but the t method is generally straightforward and accounts for estimating the SD.

Margin of error is not the standard error. SE measures estimated sampling variability in the statistic. ME scales that SE by the confidence-level critical value. At 95%, for example, ME is about 1.96 SE for z or t* SE for t.

What happens when you change n?

Increasing n reduces SE because the denominator √n grows. That usually narrows the interval. But returns diminish: moving from n = 25 to n = 100 halves SE; moving from n = 100 to n = 400 halves it again.

SE ∝ 1/√n   so   ME ∝ 1/√n (when other inputs stay fixed)

What happens when confidence rises?

A higher confidence level uses a larger critical value. If the sample stays the same, ME increases and the interval becomes wider. You cannot get higher confidence for free: you trade precision for a higher long-run capture rate.

08
A quick reasoning check

Common confusion traps

These are the distinctions that most often change a correct calculation into a misleading conclusion.

“The 95% CI contains 95% of the customers.”

No. It estimates a population parameter, such as a mean. Use a prediction interval or other method for ranges of individual outcomes.

“95% probability means this fixed mean has a 95% chance of being here.”

In the standard frequentist interpretation, the parameter is fixed. The procedure has 95% long-run coverage; this particular interval either contains the parameter or it does not.

“A large sample automatically fixes bad sampling.”

No. More observations can reduce random sampling error, but cannot automatically remove selection bias, nonresponse bias, measurement error, or an incomplete sampling frame.

“Standard deviation and standard error are interchangeable.”

No. SD describes spread among data values; SE describes spread of a statistic across repeated samples. For a mean, estimated SE = s/√n.

“A 95% interval has a 5% chance of being wrong because 5% of data are outside.”

The 5% refers to the long-run fraction of intervals that miss the parameter—not the fraction of data values outside the interval.

“The CLT says the original data become normal when n is large.”

No. The CLT concerns the sampling distribution of the sample mean (or an appropriately standardized sum), not the shape of the raw observations.

“Every observation must be independent because the sample is large.”

No. Dependence can persist at any sample size. For example, many orders from the same store may be correlated. The analysis may need cluster-aware methods.

“A wider interval means the estimate is worse.”

Not necessarily. It may honestly reflect a smaller sample, more variability, or a higher confidence target. Width communicates precision, not whether the estimate is biased.

Before reporting a confidence interval, ask

  1. What population and parameter am I trying to describe?
  2. How were the observations selected, and who or what may be missing?
  3. Are observations independent, or is there clustering or time dependence?
  4. What are the sample estimate, sample size, and variability?
  5. Is the z or t method appropriate, and what assumptions does it need?
  6. Can I explain the confidence level as long-run coverage rather than a probability claim about this fixed parameter?
